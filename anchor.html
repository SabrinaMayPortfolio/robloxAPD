00 Problem

The resources exist. Students can't find, sequence, or trust them.

  1. 6+

    tools students used in a typical week of searching

  2. 8 of 8

    described constantly feeling behind

  3. Once

    several had used UMSI career resources only at orientation

  4. 2 of 8

    had ever messaged an alum, though all called mentorship critical

How might we

How might we design a single, trustworthy surface that lets a UMSI student see what's available, who can help, and what to do next, without leaving the page?

So what

UMSI didn't have a discovery problem. It had a coherence problem, and it hit students with less time and fewer connections hardest.

01 Research

We listened to students, staff, and the tools students already use.

  • Stakeholder interviews

    Engaged Learning, Career Development, and DEI offices: 3 offices, 5 staff.

  • Student interviews (I led)

    8 UMSI students across tracks, screened for career stage and visa status, 15 to 20 minutes each over Zoom.

  • Competitive analysis

    Handshake, CareerLink, LinkedIn, Iris alumni search, and peer-school platforms: 5 scored.

  • Journey mapping (me)

    Five stages with emotion overlays and pain points.

So what

Each office's tool worked alone. Together they formed a maze.

02 Synthesis

Four themes became the team's shared vocabulary.

  • Fragmentation

    “I find the UMSI resources difficult to navigate.”

  • Mentorship opacity

    “I try and find alumni with similar career trajectories so I can follow their footsteps.”

  • Visa and identity invisibility

    International students filtered sponsoring employers by hand.

  • Resource avoidance

    “Last time I used UMSI career resources was when they first introduced them to us during orientation.”

03 Ideation

Three principles decided every screen review.

  • Trustworthy by default

  • Lower the activation cost

    Every first action in under three taps and one minute.

  • Respect the search

    Optimize for focused sessions, not engagement metrics or notification load.

Decision
Mentorship is a layer across every surface, not a standalone tab.
Why
Every student named mentorship as critical, but only 2 of 8 had reached out. The blocker was knowing who to ask and how.
Tradeoff
Mentorship stops being one destination, so it has to show up wherever it's relevant.

04 Final design

Four surfaces, one platform, every change traced to testing.

Four surfaces

Feed

A low-pressure career home that doesn't feel like LinkedIn.

Why it works this way
“Would a UMSI student feel comfortable posting this?” No engagement metrics in students' faces.

Events

One calendar across three offices, with office filters and calendar sync.

Why it works this way
Deadlines slipped through three separate calendars.

Jobs and referrals

A sponsorship signal on every card and a “what they look for” field.

Why it works this way
International students filtered sponsors by hand.

Mentorship, my flow

Filter alumni, read student-rated helpfulness, send a request from a starter template, and book directly.

Why it works this way
Only 2 of 8 had ever reached out. The blank cold email was the barrier.

I owned this flow end to end.

Three changes from testing

Testing findings and the design change each one drove
What brokeSourceWhat it changed
The Resources tab felt opaque; use dropped after orientation Interviews and testing Replaced with a social feed using familiar patterns, to make the home feel familiar.
Long job lists with no way to narrow them Usability testing Prominent search and UMSI-tuned filters, to reduce cognitive load when narrowing roles.
Referrals were “a thing I heard about but couldn't find” Usability testing Referrals got their own surface with a “what they look for” field.

06 Reflection

The real deliverable was a shared map of why it felt broken.

  1. 1

    Centralization is a trust problem.

    It's service design about ownership and the tools you're competing with, not a feature.

  2. 2

    Listen for their language.

    Students described cognitive load before I ever used the term.

  3. 3

    Templates beat policies.

    Mentorship works when it's easy to start, not when it's heavily structured.

Next time, I'd build the journey map and principles with stakeholders, not for them.

Where I've applied it since

  • Lesson: listen for their language

    I led TEA's affinity map

    Grouping participants' own words turned five contextual inquiry sessions into 17 requirements.

  • Lesson: lower the activation cost

    I removed the blank prompt in TEA

    Like Anchor's mentor templates removed the blank cold email, TEA's highlight-to-transform let users act without writing a prompt.

  • Lesson: templates beat policies

    Next

    Next: first connections that feel safe

    A player's first message or first group join should take a few taps, with guidance that makes reaching out feel easy and safe.
